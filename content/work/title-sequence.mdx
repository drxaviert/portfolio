## The concept

The commission was ninety seconds of opening credits for a series about the people who keep a city running between midnight and the first train. The reference reel was loud. The show was not.

We watched the first cut without sound twice. The second time, the thing that held was the window: one lit rectangle in a dark facade, repeated at different scales. It gave us a rule we could defend. Every frame either showed someone at work or showed the window they had left.

<Figure caption="The window, at three scales." />

## The system

A title sequence is a small system whether or not you plan one. Ours was a grid of nine windows, one transition language and a fixed hold time for each card.

The transitions were the whole design. We allowed four: a cut, a push through the window frame, a light turning on, and a slow dissolve that we used exactly once, at the end.

<Note n={1}>Four transitions, one of which appears once. Everything else is a cut.</Note>

## The process

The first assembly ran two minutes ten. It had six ideas in it, each one asking for attention. We screened it for three people who had no connection to the show and asked only what they remembered. All three described the same shot, which was not the one we had spent the week on.

That answer set the edit. We cut the sequence to a single argument: the city goes quiet, the windows stay lit, the shift changes. The version that shipped is shorter than the brief and better for it.

<Brief>Shorter than the brief. One idea, held.</Brief>

## The outcome

The sequence aired with the first episode and was never revised. The lesson was about scale: the same few decisions, repeated at every size, until the sequence stopped needing an introduction.

<Note n={2}>Delivered as one 4K master and a vertical cut for the trailer.</Note>

The best note came from the director, who said she had stopped thinking of it as a title sequence and started thinking of it as the first scene.
